hello

I use code to make things tell a story online. I trained in graphic design at FIT, including web design and development, and I'm about to finish my M.S. in data analysis & visualization. that training came hands-on, before AI was everywhere, so now it lets me use AI as a tool and keep learning its best methods. what I'm proudest of: knowing that any software I want to learn, or any project I want to build, is possible for me.

why doordash

after covid, I would dash whenever I came home from college. I learned so much more about my community, got to know a lot of new streets, and caught a flash of everyone's life going on besides mine.

one delivery stuck with me. a young man opened the door of a beautiful home, a bunch of kids laughing inside. he'd ordered crumbl cookies, and I smiled all the way back to my car thinking of how excited those kids must be, snuggled up somewhere (or bouncing off the walls, I don't know).

I can't count the hours of sunset-watching and podcast time dashing gave me, so I feel a real connection. working with the team that made that possible could only sharpen my skills.

a fresh eye

as a customer, I love the convenience. as a UI designer, I noticed the first time I ordered that the cart can get hard to find: click off a restaurant and you might not realize you still have things in it as you keep scrolling. the food imagery alongside red is a powerful combination, and I'd love to help make the path from opening the app to checkout feel seamless. the customer app is where I'd most like to start, learning the team's best practices while bringing a fresh eye.

case study: AI in my process

anyone can make a prototype now. taste is the rare part.

AI has made it so that many people can make digital prototypes, but it has rarified having taste in design. using my design and data background, I work with AI (I use claude code) to develop something that not only works but also looks well-designed. when an idea I created shows up working on the screen, it only fuels more ideas. it feels like whatever I set my mind to, I can accomplish. here's how one piece, the NYC 311 map, evolved.

  1. 01from static to story

    my original tableau workbook was static. now the story unfolds, part by part, under a user’s touch as they scroll. it helps them feel connected to the information they’re consuming and keeps it bite-sized.

  2. 02a map underneath

    the first version had no map underneath, only zip codes pinned in space. so I had map line art fade in beneath to keep things understandable.

  3. 03spikes become lines

    I asked for the solid, round-capped lines from my dissertation timeline, in the 311 colors.

  4. 04made for a phone

    on my phone the map was strangely aligned with the bottom of the screen and very small. it didn’t have the same punch as the desktop version, so I added zoom controls.

  5. 05labels that matter

    the three tallest lines now show their zip code for every complaint type, so the story reads at a glance.

  6. 06an honest scale

    AI tends to hallucinate unless you prompt it directly to fix an issue. since we were working with data, I didn’t want anything misconstrued to the public, so I went through every screen and picked apart what wasn’t the most honest way to display information. the all-four view had given each type its own scale, making 47 mold complaints look as tall as 37,141 heat complaints. now one shared scale keeps every line comparable.

what stays mine

the creative process. I never use auto AI website builders that make the product from A to Z. my process is formed by my fine arts discipline and data education, and once I have a project 60% built out with my own tools, that’s when I start working with AI to bring it to the next level. and finicky attention to detail: claude doesn’t open the laptop at 4am because one of the buttons is overlapping something it shouldn’t. I do. (every number on this site comes straight from my tableau workbooks, too.)

also built this way

remi’s clickable prototype. remi was originally a problem I had, before it was sketches or wireframes. I’ve used photo-cleaning apps before, and none of them felt trustworthy or smooth. I felt like a cash cow for pop-up advertisements. I started sketching out ideas and rambling to myself in my notes app on the train. when I got home, I put my notes into AI and had it help me come up with some preliminary ideas. then I launched adobe illustrator and wireframed every screen, like I would’ve done before AI. that gave me a reliable backbone, made by me and me only. I wanted a clickable version because you don’t know how the user flow will go until you’ve experienced it yourself. the flow wasn’t as smooth as it looked in the flats, so I reworked parts of the prototype until it led smoothly from A to B. try remi →

how I work

the very first thing I do is open procreate on my iPad, pick up my apple pencil and thumbnail-sketch in 2B pencil. sometimes the good ideas come out right away, but more often they're a few sketches down the road. it takes a moment to turn data into a mental image, let alone one that fits the brand and the user experience.

I share my first sketches, then share again once wireframes and layout blocking are down. once I'm designing, I squeeze the project until nothing I know is left to do, then ask for critique. one pair of eyes staring at anything gets confused fast (let's blame it on the troxler effect), so I always show a friend or colleague.

a trick I use

while designing layouts for the new york public library in school, I swapped every visual on the page for its opposite: dark became light, large became small, and so on. it showed me the trap the design had been sitting in and gave it a fresh new life.

what I bring

formal training in two fields. at FIT: product design, UI/UX design, motion graphics, adobe creative cloud, touchdesigner and telling a story throughout. in my M.S.: code, cursor and other AI methodologies, observable framework, data bias, data governance, gamification & ethics, the quantified self, and studying weekly under a data visualization designer at netflix.

years of brutal art critiques taught me how to take advice, trust me. have a german professor and you will learn how to grow (we loved her).

I know figma from real work: at joanna vargas the company computers only had figma, no adobe creative cloud, so that's where I designed the original spa girl digest. I later moved it to illustrator, and I find a lot of the two similar.

plenty of designers have started creating with AI. the skills baked in underneath are what shape how I use these tools, how I understand the way a human audience receives a message, and my thrill to create.
