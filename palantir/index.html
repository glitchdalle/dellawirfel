if I encounter a new data csv I haven’t seen before, I load it into a viewer and dissect the rows and columns, as well as any data dictionary that came with it. I load the head of the dataset in my notebook and poke around until I have a feel for the corpus.

from raw data to a walkable story

  1. 01from static to story

    my original tableau workbook was static. now the story unfolds, part by part, under a user’s touch as they scroll. it helps them feel connected to the information they’re consuming and keeps it bite-sized.

  2. 02a map underneath

    the first version had no map underneath, only zip codes pinned in space. so I had map line art fade in beneath to keep things understandable.

  3. 03spikes become lines

    I asked for the solid, round-capped lines from my dissertation timeline, in the 311 colors.

  4. 04made for a phone

    on my phone the map was strangely aligned with the bottom of the screen and very small. it didn’t have the same punch as the desktop version, so I added zoom controls.

  5. 05labels that matter

    the three tallest lines now show their zip code for every complaint type, so the story reads at a glance.

  6. 06an honest scale

    the all-four view had given each type its own scale, making 47 mold complaints look as tall as 37,141 heat complaints. now one shared scale keeps every line comparable.

questioning the output

AI tends to hallucinate unless you prompt it directly to fix an issue. since we were working with data, I didn’t want anything misconstrued to the public, so I went through every screen and picked apart what wasn’t the most honest way to display information.

when I push back

I push back on what I feel is done for quantity’s sake instead of quality. I ask questions when my work could be more honest and equitable. I prioritize user experience and how people move through the product: even one catch can cheapen the look and cost us trust from our users.
