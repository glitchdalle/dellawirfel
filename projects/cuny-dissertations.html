topic modeling 16,738 dissertation abstracts across 45 years.

overview

I trained an LDA topic model on 16,738 CUNY graduate center dissertation abstracts spanning 45 years, then tracked how the popularity of topics shifted over time. the approach was exploratory rather than keyword-driven: the model surfaced recurring patterns on its own, and from 25 topics I chose six to look into.

reading the circles

what you're seeing. each circle is one topic the model found. its area is that topic's average weight for the year shown: across every abstract from that year, the share of the text the model assigned to that topic, on average. sizes use the annual values from the tableau workbook, not the three-year rolling average in the dashboard, so they jump around from year to year. the timeline runs from 2000 to 2023, the last year with annual data.

the pp number. under each circle is the change in percentage points between the 2000–2009 average and the 2017–2024 average, the comparison used to pick these six. it compares two decades rather than two single years, so it won't equal the difference between the first and last circle.

what stands out.

  • social, race & community roughly tripled, from 3.68% in 2000 to 11.15% in 2023, its highest year.
  • literary, music & cultural works was the heaviest of the six every year until 2021, when social, race & community overtook it. it did so again in 2023.
  • chemical & material processes roughly halved, from 7.60% to 3.91%, with a low of 2.90% in 2022.
  • education & schools and politics, state & policy both peaked in 2017.

method

topic selection was data-driven: the three topics with the largest increases in average topic weight and the three with the largest decreases, comparing two well-populated periods (2000–2009 and 2017–2024). each dissertation gets a probability distribution across all 25 topics, so aggregating those weights by year reveals shifts that aren't obvious from any single document.

visualization

a bold three-year rolling average shows each topic's trajectory, while a pale line keeps the annual values. the top words sit under each human-readable label so viewers can see the evidence behind my interpretation. tableau turns raw, fairly inaccessible LDA output into something a nontechnical viewer can explore.

limitations

LDA detects statistical patterns in words; it doesn't understand meaning. labels are my interpretation, 25 topics was a modeling choice, preprocessing shapes what can emerge, and early years (pre-1980) are too sparse for reliable comparison. topic weight is average probability, not a share of dissertations. the results are a starting point for closer reading, not proof of why things changed.
